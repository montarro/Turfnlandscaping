# How to make a narrow side yard useful

The strip down the side of the house is often the last part of a block anyone thinks about, and the first to turn into a corridor of bins, weeds and mud. It is also the route everything takes to the backyard: people, dogs, wheelbarrows, the mower and, during any landscaping, the materials. Getting it right is mostly about deciding what has to pass through it, then choosing a surface and planting that can cope. This guide covers those decisions and the site checks to make before you change anything.

## Decide what has to pass through

Before thinking about how it looks, list what the side yard has to do:

- **People.** Is it the main way from the front to the back? Does it need to be walkable in the wet, at night, with hands full?
- **Bins.** Where do they live, and where do they roll to on collection day? A bin needs a firm, level surface and a gate wide enough for it.
- **Equipment.** The mower, the wheelbarrow and anything coming in for future work. A gate narrower than a wheelbarrow turns every job in the backyard into a hand-carry.
- **Services.** Gas and water meters, the hot water unit, air-conditioning units, drains, downpipes and inspection points usually live down the side. They have to stay accessible, and nothing should be built over them.
- **Dogs.** If this is the dog's run, see our guide on [turf for a yard with dogs](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-turf-dogs-melbourne); it changes the surface decision.

## Check levels and drainage before covering anything

Side yards collect water. The house roof drains to downpipes along the side, the neighbour's ground often sits higher, and the strip itself may fall towards the house rather than away from it. Covering a wet side yard with pavers, turf or synthetic grass does not fix the water; it hides it.

Before changing levels or laying a new surface, look at where water goes in heavy rain, where the downpipes discharge, and whether the ground falls away from the house. If the strip holds water, or the levels near the house need to change, have a competent professional look at the drainage first. Changing ground levels against a house wall, or covering its weep holes, can cause damp problems inside. The [Building and Plumbing Commission](https://www.bpc.vic.gov.au/home-owners/home-renovation-and-building-essentials/landscaping) makes the same point about paving work redirecting water flow, and suggests checking with your council or water authority on local requirements.

## Continuous surface or spaced path?

**A continuous surface**, such as paving or compacted gravel, is worth considering for bins, barrows and regular foot traffic. Compare how easily your equipment rolls over each finish, how the surface will drain, and what upkeep it needs. Allow for weeding and cleaning rather than assuming a hard surface needs no maintenance. A fully paved strip can also look like a corridor.

**A spaced path**, stepping stones through pebbles, turf or ground cover, suits a side yard that is mainly walked, where you want some green, and where the budget is tighter. Stones through pebbles still drain well and still take a wheelbarrow if the stones are set level and close enough together. Our guide to [pavers versus stepping stones](/blog/pavers-vs-stepping-stones-garden-path) compares the two in more detail, and our [stepping-stone paths](/services/stepping-stone-paths) and [paving](/services/paving) pages cover how each is built.

**Lawn** in a side yard works where the strip gets some sun and is not the main traffic route. A narrow strip in full shade with the dog running up and down it will not stay a lawn. Where there is enough light, natural turf gives a side yard the same green as the backyard, which is what the job at the top of this article shows.

**Synthetic turf** is a common answer for a dark, narrow, heavily used strip. It needs the same drainage thought as anything else, and it gets hot in direct sun.

## Planting for a narrow space

Side yards are often shaded on one side by the house and on the other by the fence, with a band of sun that moves across through the day. Choose plants by their mature width, not their size in the pot. A shrub that grows to a metre and a half wide can close a narrow path within a few seasons. Narrow, upright plants along the fence, climbers on a trellis, or a raised sleeper bed along one side keep the walkway clear and the green up at eye level. Our [plants, garden beds and mulch](/services/plants-garden-beds-mulch) page covers the planting side.

A raised bed along the fence also solves another side-yard problem: it lifts the planting above the compacted, rubble-filled ground that many side strips have, and it gives the lawn or path a clean edge.

## A real side yard: turf and a raised bed

The side yard at the top of this article had been bare, mounded soil beside a timber-edged raised bed along the fence. It was levelled and laid with natural turf between the raised sleeper bed on the fence side and the house on the other, leaving the gravel strip and the bin on the house side clear.

## Before you start

1. List everything that has to pass through, including on bin day and during future work.
2. Measure the clear width at the narrowest point, usually a gate, a meter box or an air-conditioning unit.
3. Watch where the water goes in rain and check which way the ground falls.
4. Find every service and keep it accessible.
5. Decide the main route and put the hard surface there, with planting and lawn where they will not be walked on.
