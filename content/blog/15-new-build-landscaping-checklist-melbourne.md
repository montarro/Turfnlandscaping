# Landscaping a new build after handover in Melbourne

Many new homes are handed over with the house finished and the block not. What you get outside is usually bare or compacted ground, builders' leftovers and a few things sticking out of the soil that matter later. This checklist covers what to collect before handover, what to check on the block, and how to brief a landscaper so the yard is built in the right order.

## Before handover: collect the documents

Ask the builder for, or find in your contract:

- **The site plan and any engineering or soil report.** These show the finished levels the builder worked to, the soil class, and where the house drains run.
- **Where the services are.** Stormwater, sewer, water, gas, power and data all cross the block somewhere. The inspection points and meters are visible; the runs between them are not.
- **What the builder's contract includes outside.** Some contracts include a driveway, a path to the door, a clothesline or basic fencing. Others include nothing else. Knowing exactly where the builder's work stops is the first step in scoping the landscaping.
- **Any estate or council requirements.** Some estates have design guidelines covering front fences, driveways and what the front garden must look like by a certain date. Check the documents for your own estate and council rather than assuming the rules from another estate apply to you.

## On the block: what to look at

**Compaction.** Machinery has usually been driving over the block for months, so the ground near the house and along the access side is often compacted hard. Topsoil cannot simply be spread over it and planted. The compacted layer needs to be broken up and dealt with, or water will sit on it.

**Leftover material.** Broken bricks, mortar, offcuts, plastic and render often end up buried in the top layer. This is common, but it has to come out before beds and lawn go in.

**Soil.** What is on the surface after a build is often not garden soil. Imported topsoil is part of many new-build landscaping jobs, and where the natural ground is heavy clay the soil needs improving, not just covering.

**Levels and drainage.** The builder set the finished floor level and shaped the ground around the house to drain away from it. Landscaping must keep it that way. Raising garden beds against the house, covering weep holes, or changing where water runs can cause damp problems, so any change to levels or drainage near the house should be checked with the relevant professional. The [Building and Plumbing Commission](https://www.bpc.vic.gov.au/home-owners/home-renovation-and-building-essentials/landscaping) notes that paving and driveway work can interfere with drainage, and suggests asking your council or water authority about local requirements.

**Existing services.** Risers, inspection openings and meters must stay accessible. In the photo at the top of this article the service pipe inside the new planter was left standing clear while the wall and soil went in.

## Plan access before the fences go up

On a new block the easiest time to get soil, turf, crushed rock and materials in is before side fences, gates and finished paths restrict the way through. If fencing is part of the builder's work or an estate deadline, plan the heavy landscaping deliveries around it, or at least leave a gate wide enough for a wheelbarrow and a small machine. Access is one of the main cost drivers in [landscaping a Melbourne home](/blog/landscaping-cost-melbourne).

## Know which work counts as building work

In Victoria, landscaping carried out in association with the construction of a home is domestic building work. That matters in two ways:

- When the contract price is over $10,000, the work must be done by an appropriately registered building practitioner under a major domestic building contract.
- Retaining structures, decks attached to the house, verandahs, attached pergolas and most fences are generally domestic building work; planting lawns and trees, garden beds, paving and driveways are not.

A retaining wall 1 metre or higher, or one on or near a boundary where a neighbouring property could be damaged, needs a building permit in any case. The [BPC landscaping page](https://www.bpc.vic.gov.au/home-owners/home-renovation-and-building-essentials/landscaping) sets all of this out with worked examples, and our guide explains the signs that [your yard needs a retaining wall](/blog/does-yard-need-retaining-wall-melbourne).

## Build it in the right order

The order is the same as for any [complete landscape transformation](/services/complete-landscape-transformations): clear and dispose, fix levels and structure, lay surfaces, then soil, turf, beds and planting. On a new build it is tempting to lay turf first so the block looks finished. If a wall, a path or a drainage fix comes later, that turf gets dug up.

[Turf preparation, levelling and drainage](/services/turf-preparation-levelling-drainage) on a new build is often more work than on an established garden, for all the reasons above.

## The landscaping brief: what to have ready

- Handover date and when you can give access.
- The site plan, soil report and anything the builder provided about drainage.
- Photos of the block from the street and from the house, including the side access.
- What the builder's contract includes outside, and any estate deadlines.
- How you want to use the space: lawn, entertaining, parking, low upkeep.
- A rough budget, and whether staging the work suits you.
